# Agwu: The Spirit of Divination and Healing in Igbo Religion — Episode 1

Source manuscript by Zebedee Ejike Korie. Adapted for narration by Emeka.

---

Nno — welcome. Today we're walking into one of the deepest rooms of Igbo spirituality: the spirit called Agwu.

If you grew up around Igbo elders, you may have heard the name spoken low — the way people speak of power they respect and do not want to provoke. Agwu is the deity of divination and healing. It is the force behind the Dibia, the traditional healer and seer. And it is a spirit of two faces: the one that blesses, and the one that unsettles.

Agwu is as old as Odinani itself — the traditional religion of the Igbo, built around Chukwu, the supreme being, and a family of deities who govern different corners of life. Agwu's corner is knowledge: the hidden knowledge of divination, and the healing knowledge of medicine. For centuries, Agwu has spoken to people through the Dibia, the diviner who reads signs the rest of us cannot see.

The myths around Agwu teach the lesson of balance. In one story, Agwu grants extraordinary knowledge to a diviner — then turns and confuses the very same person. Because the gift and the burden come from the same source. That is the message: to carry spiritual power, you must stay in right relationship with it.

Agwu's two faces are not good versus evil in a simple way. The benevolent Agwu gives wealth, fertility, good fortune, and health to those who honour it. But the same Agwu, when neglected or disrespected, is said to bring sickness, misfortune, and mental disturbance. This is why Agwu is never ignored. In the Igbo understanding of the world, health is spiritual first — and physical medicine alone rarely finishes the work.

Agwu shows itself in different forms, each with its own work: Agwu Nsi, tied to divination; Agwu Afa, the diviner's own spirit; Agwu Omumu, tied to fertility. Each form has its own rituals, its own offerings, its own careful protocols.

Worship of Agwu is done through offerings — animals, food, sacred items — and it is the Dibia who leads these ceremonies. The Dibia is the bridge: trained in divination, deep in herbal medicine, able to diagnose what is spiritually wrong, and prescribe the remedy. This is not superstition to the people who practise it. It is a working system of diagnosis and care, passed down for generations.

Divination is how the Dibia hears Agwu. One of the oldest methods is Afa — the casting of seeds, cowries, and beads, and the reading of the patterns they make. Through Afa, the Dibia asks Agwu what is wrong, and what must be done. This is how the spiritual cause of an illness is found, and how the right herbs and the right ritual are chosen.

And here is where Agwu and healing meet most clearly: herbal medicine. The Dibia does not separate the spiritual from the physical. A treatment is herbs and ritual together. There are common plants, each with a known purpose, and they are chosen according to what divination reveals. Modern practitioners still carry these methods, and many blend them with contemporary care.

Agwu is not only a private matter. It shapes families, communities, festivals, and the great passages of life — birth, marriage, death. To honour Agwu at these moments is to keep the community in balance with the seen and the unseen.

So what does Agwu mean today? For many Igbo people, it is still a living presence — a reminder that knowledge is a gift that must be carried carefully, that healing is more than medicine, and that the old ways still speak. Whether you study it as tradition, as history, or as living faith, Agwu stands at the centre of how the Igbo have understood divination, healing, and the balance of life.

Daalu. Thank you for walking this path with me. If this moved you, share it — and carry the names of the old spirits with respect.
